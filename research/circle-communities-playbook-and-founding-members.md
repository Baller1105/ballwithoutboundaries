## Part 2: What They All Have in Common

1. **Nobody started from zero.** Every one of them grew out of an existing audience: a free Facebook group (Exit Five), dinners (TroopHR), course students (Josh Hall), a podcast (SPI), a recruiting network (MacGregor). **The community was the second product, not the first.**
2. **The founder is the face, at least at first.** Daily posts, voice notes, personal welcomes. Dave Gerhardt expected 20–30 members and showed up every day anyway.
3. **Growth came from referrals, not ads.** TroopHR gets 60% of members from referrals, Outlier is "word of mouth," and Exit Five grew through the founder's own content. I found no paid ads as a main channel in any of these.
4. **A narrow "who it's for."** B2B marketers. HR leaders. Web designers. They had a clear identity first and broadened later.
5. **Live events are the heartbeat.** Recurring calls online plus in-person gatherings (Exit Five: 30+ a year; Outlier: 200+ a year; TroopHR started *as* dinners).
6. **Started cheap or free, then raised prices.** Exit Five went free → $499 → more. Outlier went $480 → $600 → $249/mo. Founders were rewarded and newcomers paid more.
7. **One home.** SPI and Exit Five both grew faster after consolidating scattered groups into a single Circle space.

## Part 3: Six Things That Make a Community Sticky

### 1. Members know each other, not just the founder
The strongest retention happens when **members start relying on one another**. Josh Hall: people join for the content and stay for the community. **Design for member-to-member friendships**: pair new members with a "buddy," run small groups, and introduce people by name.

### 2. The first 7 days are everything
Circle's data: "onboarding is retention," and **fixing the first 30 days fixes most churn**. Best practice is a personal welcome (video or DM), a question about their goal, their first public post within 48 hours, and a connection to one other member. **Four Seasons check-in energy.**

### 3. Rituals people can set their clock by
A weekly live call on the same day and time. Monthly challenges. An annual retreat. Rituals become a habit, and a habit is hard to cancel.

### 4. Progress you can see (accountability)
SPI's top tier is built on **cohorts, accountability and office hours**. People stay when the community helps them *get somewhere*. For ONE BILL this is a natural fit: "**the bill you killed this month**" is visible, celebratable progress.

### 5. Recognition and identity
Outlier: membership card, Hall of Fame, public welcomes. TroopHR: 85% would be "very disappointed" without it, because it's part of who they are. Use **founding-member badges, wins shared publicly and member spotlights**.

### 6. In real life, even occasionally
Every top community adds in-person time: dinners, retreats, meetups. One in-person event can be worth a year of online engagement.

## Part 4: Free Founding Members, Paid New Members, and How to Present It

### The research is clear: this is a respected, standard model
Guides call it the **"Grandfather Method"**: existing members keep free access *as a reward for being founders*, and the fee applies only to people who join after a set date. The one thing every source warns against: **never surprise a free group with "everyone must pay now."** That is what breaks trust. You're doing the opposite.

### The single most important move: tell them the plan on day one
The "am I using them?" feeling disappears when **there's no hidden agenda**. When you invite them, say plainly:
- It's free for you, **for life**.
- Later we'll open it to the public at a paid price.
- You are getting in free *because* you're helping build it.

Someone who says yes to that has given informed consent. You're not taking advantage of them; you're making them an offer they accepted with full information.

### Sample founding-member invitation

> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm building something I care deeply about: **ONE BILL**, a community helping Americans take control of their money one bill at a time.
>
> I'm hand-picking [25–50] Founding Members, and you're one of the first people I thought of because [specific, genuine reason].
>
> **Here's the deal, fully transparent:**
> - Your membership is **free for life**. No catch, no "trial."
> - You'll get the **Founding Member** badge and a permanent place on our Founders Wall.
> - You'll have a real say in what we build, through a private Founders channel and a direct line to me.
> - Later in [month], we'll open ONE BILL to the public at $[X]/month. **You'll never pay.**
>
> What I'm asking: show up, share what you know when it feels right, and tell me honestly what's working and what isn't.
>
> Would you be one of our founders?
>
> [Your name]

### Sample announcement when you open to paid members

> Founders, big news: ONE BILL opens to the public on [date] at $[X]/month.
>
> **Nothing changes for you.** You're founders: free for life, exactly as promised.
>
> Here's why paid membership matters *to you*: it funds the things you asked for, like [more live sessions, the retreat, expert guests]. New members will also find a community you built, which is why it's worth paying for.
>
> One ask: if you know someone who needs ONE BILL, your personal invite means more than any ad. As a thank-you, [founders get X for every referral].

## Part 5: "Am I Using Them?" Bridging the Value Gap

**First, the honest answer: is it really a thing?** Sometimes, but only in specific situations. People feel used when:
- The plan is **hidden** and they find out later (fix: tell them upfront).
- Their content or expertise is **packaged and sold without credit** (fix: name them and spotlight them).
- They're asked to **work** (teach, run sessions) with nothing back (fix: pay them, share revenue, or give them a real title).
- Their free access gets **taken away later** (fix: "for life" means for life).

Avoid those four and the research suggests founders feel **honored, not used**. Exit Five and Josh Hall's founding members are still there years later and are the biggest fans.

### Reframes for the voice that says "I'm taking from them"

1. **They're not giving you their time. They're getting a free seat at something others will pay for.** Every paying member who joins makes their free membership *more* valuable, because the room gets bigger and better.
2. **Charging new members is how you protect the founders.** Free communities die because the founder burns out or runs out of money. Revenue keeps the lights on for the people who got in free.
3. **You're the host, not the taker.** At the Ritz-Carlton, the host who arranges the room, the introductions and the experience creates the value. You bring people together who would never have met. That connection is the product, and you made it.
4. **Your story is the qualification.** You don't need to be the smartest money person in the room; you need to be the one who got everyone *into* the room. MacGregor didn't win by being the top expert. He won by being the connector.
5. **Let the evidence answer the doubt.** One research source suggests asking: "Can I point to evidence that contradicts this doubt?" After 30 days, ask founders: "What has ONE BILL done for you?" Their answers will be your proof, in their words.

### Ways to make the value exchange visibly fair (pick 3–4)
- **Free for life**, in writing.
- **Founding Member badge** and a Founders Wall: permanent recognition.
- **Founders Council**: a real vote on topics, features and the retreat location.
- **Spotlights**: feature founders in content and on LinkedIn, which gives *them* exposure.
- **Pay contributors**: if a founder teaches a session, offer an honorarium or a revenue share.
- **Referral reward**: a gift, swag or revenue credit for each paid member they bring in.
- **First access**: founders get every new event or program first and free.

## Part 6: ONE BILL Launch Plan (90 Days)

| Phase | When | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Hand-pick founders** | Weeks 1–2 | Personally invite 25–50 people using the script above. Use 1:1 messages, not a mass post. |
| **2. Build with them** | Weeks 3–8 | Weekly live call (same day and time), a "kill one bill" challenge, a buddy pairing for everyone, a public welcome post for every founder. |
| **3. Collect proof** | Week 8 | Ask founders: "What has this done for you?" Gather testimonials and bill-savings wins. |
| **4. Announce paid** | Weeks 9–10 | Founders hear first. Set a public launch date with a launch price locked for life for the first 100 paid members. |
| **5. Open doors** | Weeks 11–12 | Founders invite friends (with a referral reward). Founder testimonials go on the sales page. |

**Target:** about 10% monthly churn or better at launch, dropping toward Circle's average of 5–7% as onboarding matures. Remember: **a few hundred paying members is a real business.**
